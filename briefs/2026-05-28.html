Section 01

Markets

The Index Holds at ATH While Sentiment Collapses Beneath It
Wednesday’s session produced the most significant divergence of the recovery: the S&P 500 was essentially flat (–0.02%), holding near its all-time high at approximately 7,505, while Fear & Greed crashed to 22—back into Extreme Fear for the first time since mid-April. The index went from neutral (50) to Extreme Fear (22) in approximately two weeks without a meaningful drawdown in price. The divergence reflects a market where the headline index is supported by concentrated tech and discretionary strength while the underlying positioning is deteriorating. Discretionary (+1.76%) and staples (+1.14%) led Wednesday; tech (–0.38%), energy (–1.49%), and financials (–0.83%) lagged. UVXY fell 3.25% even as the ceasefire unravelled—the options market is not pricing the strike exchange as a systemic event. Futures are near 7,538 in Thursday premarket, modestly higher.
Oil and Gold Both Fall on Rate Hike Fears — The War Is Now an Inflation Event
The most counterintuitive move: oil fell 4.36% (WTI) and 3.69% (Brent) on a day the US and Iran traded air strikes. Gold dropped 1.33% to a two-month low. Silver plunged 3.18%. The traditional playbook says commodities should rally on escalation. Instead, the market is pricing the war’s inflationary impact as a rate hike catalyst rather than a supply disruption event. Reuters characterized gold’s decline as driven by “US-Iran tension stoking inflation and rate hike fears.” The dollar strengthened on expectations of tighter monetary policy, which compresses commodity prices denominated in dollars. For IOWN, this reframes the energy sleeve: CVX, VLO, OKE, and CTRA are no longer benefiting from a risk premium but are facing the same rate-driven headwind as every other asset class. The energy sector fell 1.49% on Wednesday despite active combat in the Strait of Hormuz.
Semi Sleeve Gives Back Gains — QCOM –6.20%, MRVL –4.59%
The semiconductor names that surged on the Trump-Xi chip export thaw and AMD earnings are now pulling back. QCOM fell 6.20% to $233.40, MRVL declined 4.59% to $198.70, and FTNT dropped 4.50%. COIN shed 3.46%. The bottom five were concentrated in the growth and tech sleeves. On the positive side, SUPV surged 9.24%, HUT gained 4.53%, STLD added 3.09%, and HOOD rose 2.89%. The rotation from growth into value and smaller names mirrors the broader market dynamic: the concentrated tech rally is pausing while the rest of the market catches up or, alternatively, the entire complex is repricing for a tighter rate environment.

Section 02

Geopolitics

Ceasefire in Name Only — Both Sides Trading Strikes Near Hormuz
The US shot down four Iranian drones and struck an Iranian ground control station near Bandar Abbas that was preparing to launch a fifth, according to CNN and ABC News. Three explosions were heard east of the strategic port city. Iran’s IRGC retaliated by targeting an unspecified US airbase in the region. Kuwait reported intercepting hostile missile and drone attacks. This marks the second time this week the US has struck Iranian targets near the Strait. The US characterized both sets of strikes as within the bounds of the ceasefire; Iran condemned them as violations. The ceasefire, originally agreed April 8 and extended indefinitely in late April, now exists as a diplomatic fiction rather than a operational reality. Reuters described the situation as the ceasefire “unravelling.” For markets, the escalation has been absorbed without a meaningful equity drawdown—the S&P held within 30 basis points of its all-time high. Three months of conflict have compressed the market’s sensitivity to each new military exchange.
The MOU Framework Stalls as Combat Resumes
The 14-point memorandum of understanding that appeared close to finalization last week has stalled. Trump stated he would not rush a deal, per CNN. Iran’s IRGC warned of further retaliation. The remaining clauses that had been described as “one or two differences” appear to have widened as military operations resumed. The Hormuz deal that Trump discussed with Xi in Beijing—which produced the Chinese vessel transit opening—has not translated into a broader reopening. The key energy and shipping trends after three months of disruption are now structural, per Reuters: supply chains have been permanently rerouted, insurance markets have repriced, and the Hormuz corridor operates under a fundamentally different risk regime than pre-February 28.

On Our Radar

1. F&G at 22 while the S&P is at ATH is the most extreme price-sentiment divergence of the entire conflict. Either price is wrong (the index should be lower) or sentiment is wrong (positioning should be higher). Historically, when this divergence resolves, it resolves toward the price signal—meaning sentiment catches up and the index holds. But the resolution requires a catalyst: either the MOU materializes or the rate hike fears ease. Without either, the divergence could persist or resolve negatively. Think Like an Owner.
2. Oil falling 4% on an escalation day signals a regime change in how the market prices the conflict. The war premium has been replaced by a rate premium. If rate hike expectations continue to build, the energy sleeve faces a different headwind than the one it was designed for. CVX, VLO, OKE, and CTRA were positioned for supply disruption; they are now facing demand destruction via tighter monetary policy. The dividend sleeve’s cash-generative names (ADP, FAST, SYK) may outperform in this regime. Research Reveals Opportunities.

AMD’s Q1 data center revenue of $5.8B (+57%), the Trump-Xi chip export thaw, and MRVL’s BofA $200 target remain intact. The pullback follows double-digit single-session gains across multiple names. For the sleeve, the question is whether the rate hike narrative overwhelms the AI capex cycle or whether the two can coexist. Historically, tech has performed through tightening cycles when secular demand is strong enough. The physical world matters.
